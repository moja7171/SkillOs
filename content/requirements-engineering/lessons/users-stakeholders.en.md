## Why we spend so much time on these two words

A product's success largely depends on how well we understood **user** and **stakeholder** wants and translated them into the solution. You've certainly bought a product expecting certain features, only to find the product (or part of it) missing, clunky, or unfriendly to use — and left a bad review. That usually means the team building it didn't really know its users and stakeholders.

## Who exactly is a "user"?

Per the ISO definition: a user is an individual or group that interacts with a system, or benefits from a system during its use.

Most people first think only of whoever is directly operating the system. But there are several kinds of users:

- **Direct/end user:** whoever experiences the system's primary mission. For an airliner, interestingly, the **passengers** are the end users — not the pilots and crew — because the plane's purpose is to move passengers, not the pilots.
- **Maintainer:** whoever keeps the system operational.
- **Tester:** whoever tests the system during development.
- **Production artisan:** whoever works with the system on the production line while it's being built — their needs matter too (e.g. a design that makes production faster/safer).
- **Adversary:** a hacker, vandal, or thief. Oddly enough, these count as "users" too, because the system's behavior has to account for their (negative) needs — i.e. prevent them.

For an ATM: the end user (the person withdrawing cash), the cash-resupply guard, the ATM owner, the ATM maintainer, the production artisan who built it in the factory, the user's bank, and a hacker/vandal.

## Stakeholder — bigger than user

Per the ISO/IEEE definition: a stakeholder is an individual or organization with a right, share, claim or interest in the system.

**Every user is a stakeholder, but not every stakeholder is a user.** The ATM's owner (who profits from transaction fees) is a stakeholder without necessarily being an end user. Or if your teenager drives, you (as a parent) are a stakeholder in their car — you care about its safety, reliability and price, without being the driver yourself.

More stakeholder examples:
- An **investor** who hears a product pitch and can profit from its success without ever using it themselves.
- A **regulatory body** like the US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA), which ensures vehicles have airbags, tire-pressure sensors and safety glass.
- The **Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)**, which oversees emission standards and materials used in vehicle construction.

## Active vs. passive stakeholders

- **Active stakeholder:** someone with a direct, conscious interest in the system — a funder, a development team member, a regulatory reviewer, the end user.
- **Passive stakeholder:** someone who doesn't necessarily care that the system exists, but the system interacts with or affects them anyway. Pedestrians walking near a busy street don't care about the capabilities of the cars around them, but they still need to be careful not to get hit. These are passive stakeholders — and we may want to design the system to catch their attention or prevent them from being harmed.

## The role of users and stakeholders in product development

Very early — before anything is designed or built — the requirements engineer talks to current and potential users and stakeholders to elicit their requirements (that's a topic a few lessons ahead, "requirements elicitation"). During development, the requirements engineer keeps collecting feedback to make sure the team is building what the user and stakeholder originally wanted — or, if the development team found a better solution, to check whether that new solution is genuinely useful too.

> Bottom line: identify users and stakeholders early. The later you identify them, the more time and energy you'll spend guessing what they want instead of actually asking.
