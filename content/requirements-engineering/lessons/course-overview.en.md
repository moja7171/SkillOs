## What this course is actually about

Imagine you're buying a car. You definitely have things in mind: safety (airbags, tire-pressure sensors), acceleration, top speed, trunk capacity, number of passengers... all of these are **requirements** you have for that car.

The same thing happens in product and system development, just more formally: a group of stakeholders has a set of wants, and we have to elicit those wants, refine them, and translate them into a language a design team can use to design, implement, integrate and test — to check whether the requirement was actually met. That's requirements engineering, and it's what this course is about.

## Course roadmap

The course roughly follows this order:

1. **Foundations** — basic definitions, the product life cycle, users and stakeholders.
2. **Scenarios and Use Cases** — before we can write requirements, we need to understand what the system is supposed to do.
3. **Requirements elicitation** — techniques and tools like interviews, workshops, questionnaires, brainstorming.
4. **Writing requirements** — how to write a requirement that's implementation-free, unambiguous, singular, complete, verifiable, feasible and consistent.
5. **Software requirements** — the software-specific version of the same process.
6. **Documentation and modeling** — specification documents and diagrams like ER, class, sequence.
7. **Requirement types** — functional, quality, constraint, interface...
8. **Statement of Work** — the document that spells out what the contractor is required to do.
9. **Common issues and negotiation** — because stakeholders disagree, always.
10. **Requirements management** — tracing a requirement from its origin through implementation, across the system's life cycle.

## The examples used in this course

To keep things concrete, there are two running examples used throughout: an **ATM** (which everyone already knows) and a **tree-seed drone** that flies over burned areas and drops seeds to help reforest them. Both have hardware and software, and both are great at illustrating every requirement category (functional, non-functional, user interface, business...).

## Where this course comes from

This course is built on **INCOSE**'s (the International Council on Systems Engineering) standard definitions, backed by over 15 years of the instructor's own experience writing and reviewing requirements — from military aviation programs to teaching systems engineering at the graduate level. Where another definition (like PMI's) is more useful, we'll bring that in too.
