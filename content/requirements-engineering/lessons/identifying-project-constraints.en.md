## What a project constraint is

A constraint is a limiting factor or condition that affects a system's or project's ability to meet its requirements and goals. It can be external (regulations, market) or internal (budget, skilled personnel).

Identifying constraints early matters a lot: it helps us make informed trade-off and design decisions. Identifying constraints late leads to unrealistic expectations, unmet requirements, project delays, and ultimately project failure.

## Two big categories of constraints

This lesson splits project constraints (which overlap with the "constraint requirements" we already saw, but focuses here at the **project** level, not just the system) into two categories:

### Resource constraints

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Budget | Limited budget; have to trade off design or component quality |
| Personnel | Limited number of skilled engineers; can affect schedule or quality |
| Equipment/tools/facilities | No access to specialized test or quality-control equipment |

Common solutions: partnering with other organizations, using open-source software or code from a previous project, training existing staff or hiring new expertise.

### Business constraints

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Technical capability | An exciting technology isn't mature/reliable enough yet |
| Regulatory | Safety standards, environmental restrictions on materials |
| Schedule | Time to market, project deadlines |
| Market | Customer preferences, competition |
| Financial | Cost targets, return on investment (ROI) |

## A couple of important notes about these constraints

**The marketing team is a stakeholder too.** Design engineers sometimes have their own idea of what the customer wants, which differs from marketing's actual research. There needs to be a clear communication channel between design and marketing, with marketing involved from the very start of the project.

**Technical-capability constraints can be surprising.** You might want to use an exciting new technology, only to find it's not mature enough yet for practical use — that's when you need to consider alternatives.

## How to identify constraints

1. **Gather information** about the system, the project, stakeholders, and external factors (regulations, market conditions).
2. Use analysis and brainstorming techniques:
   - **Interviews** with stakeholders, suppliers, experts.
   - **Brainstorming and mind mapping** to uncover overlooked constraints.
   - **SWOT analysis** (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats).
   - **PESTLE analysis** (political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental) — for factors outside the organization.
   - **Historical data analysis** from past projects — identifies recurring challenges.
3. **Categorize and prioritize** the identified constraints (by impact).
4. **Document and manage** constraints — turn them into constraint requirements, and review them regularly since they can change over the project's life.

## How to manage constraints

Once identified, there are four common ways to deal with a constraint:

- **Mitigate:** find the root cause and an alternative solution that reduces its impact.
- **Accept:** some constraints just have to be accepted, adjusting the plan (schedule, budget, scope) around them.
- **Defer:** push a feature/requirement's implementation to a later phase or project, when it can be handled better.
- **Trade study:** compare different design options by cost, schedule and technical impact to pick the best path.

A few good practices: involve every stakeholder (customer, supplier, expert, marketing and sales) in identifying constraints; define constraints **precisely and measurably**; review and update them regularly; and build a culture of constraint management (open communication across departments) in the organization.

> Bottom line: a constraint can't be ignored or worked around — but it can be managed intelligently. Early identification, categorization, and picking the right strategy (mitigate/accept/defer/trade study) is the key to success.
