## Where do requirements come from?

Before we get into how to elicit and write requirements, let's see where they actually originate. There are three main sources.

## 1. Stakeholders

As we've already seen, stakeholders span a wide range of people: end users, secondary users, the system owner, maintainers, regulatory and certification authorities, legislators, repair facilities, unrelated bystanders, and project personnel (project manager, systems engineer, design engineer, cost analyst, legal, HR — and of course, the bosses).

## 2. Documentation

Documentation related to the system or service is the second source:

- Development guidelines, standard engineering principles, manufacturing principles
- Standards, laws, regulations, statutes
- Security standards or guidelines
- Analysis and test results
- Marketing team analysis reports (customer satisfaction surveys of legacy systems, support team feedback, user reviews)
- Competing-system analysis — public reviews, generic/public specifications (not confidential details protected by intellectual property)

## 3. Legacy systems

Most products on the market (cars, helicopters, aircraft, phones, computers) are improved versions of an older design. We can use a legacy system as a starting point — keep or improve its good capabilities, and fix its shortcomings.

Good sources for this:
- Problem/trouble reports, support tickets, customer feedback forms (the best source for improvement/upgrade requirements)
- User and maintenance manuals
- Drawings and designs, software source code, logic and data flows (if you have access)
- Legacy system/stakeholder requirements specifications (if they exist)

> Practical note: as you move through this course, you'll see each of these sources covered in more detail — this lesson is just the map.
