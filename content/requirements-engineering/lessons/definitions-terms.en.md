## Why we need to agree on definitions first

If I say "apple," I hope you're picturing a fruit, not an orange — because we both share the same definition of an apple. It's the same in requirements engineering: before we go further, we need a shared vocabulary for a handful of key terms.

## Requirement

There are two well-known definitions:

| Source | Definition |
|---|---|
| INCOSE (International Council on Systems Engineering) | A statement that identifies a system/product/process characteristic or constraint, which is unambiguous, clear, unique, consistent, standalone, and verifiable, and is deemed necessary for stakeholder acceptability. |
| PMI (Project Management Institute) | A condition or capability that must be present in a product, service, or result to satisfy a business need. |

INCOSE's definition is far more thorough but heavier; PMI's is simpler. This course uses whichever fits the situation.

## Three words inside that definition that need their own explanation

**Capability:** an important product feature that justifies buying or building the product. For an ATM, "remote cash withdrawal" is a capability. Think of it as what would go on a sales brochure — the highlighted selling points are the capabilities.

**Necessary:** a capability counts as "necessary" when it's essential for the user to accomplish their mission. The opposite is easier to spot: displaying a 3D hologram of a bank teller on an ATM, however cool, is **not necessary** — the ATM can still dispense cash without it. "Unnecessary" capabilities might be great from a marketing angle, but they're not required for the system's core mission.

**Business need:** a quantifiable benefit to the organization from building a new product, offering a new service, or improving something that already exists — usually aimed at a return on investment (ROI).

## A few more terms you'll see a lot in this course

Some of these get their own dedicated lesson later (I'll point out where); here you just get a short definition so the rest of the course is easier to follow.

- **System:** an integrated set of elements, subsystems or assemblies that accomplish a defined objective. An ATM, for example, is made up of a keypad, a display, a card reader, a cash box, a vault, a central processor and a modem.
- **Product life cycle:** the path a product takes from concept to retirement. That's the very next dedicated lesson.
- **Requirements management:** a systematic approach to developing and managing requirements across a product's life cycle — including elicitation, development, refinement and organization.
- **Stakeholder:** an individual or organization with a right, share, or interest in the system. Gets its own dedicated lesson.
- **User:** an individual or group who interacts with the system or benefits from using it. A subset of stakeholders.
- **System environment:** the external entities the system interacts with in a given context — not just weather, but other vehicles, traffic lights, bystanders, and so on. Gets its own dedicated lesson.
- **System boundaries:** the line that determines whether something counts as part of the system or part of the external environment.
- **System hierarchy:** an organized representation of a system's structure using part-to-whole relationships, from the most abstract layer down to the most detailed. Gets its own dedicated lesson.
- **Systems engineering:** an interdisciplinary approach that governs the entire technical and managerial effort needed to turn stakeholder needs and constraints into a solution. Gets its own dedicated lesson.

> This lesson is just a glossary. You don't need to memorize everything right now — whichever concept has its own dedicated lesson, we'll come back to it there with more detail and examples.
