## What a requirements engineer actually does

A requirements engineer is a team member who collects and manages a product or service's requirements, advocates for the product's existence, and coordinates across different parts of the organization. This goes far beyond "going around asking each team member what their requirements are" — sometimes you have to collect thousands or tens of thousands of requirements in a short window, organize them into a database, build relationships between them, and make sure they're all well written.

## Traits of a good requirements engineer

| Trait | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Patient** | Has to work with people of very different personalities; gathering requirements takes time; has to run multiple elicitation and change-review meetings |
| **Systems thinking** | Can "see the forest through the trees" — considers each requirement's impact on the product's overall performance and ROI, not just the details right in front of them |
| **Flexible** | Can quickly adapt to unexpected situations — e.g. if a stakeholder can't make a formal meeting, switch to email or a phone call |
| **Good communicator** | Can translate people's thoughts into requirements language, mediate between stakeholders' differing views, and be persuasive at the right moments |

Systems-thinking example: for the tree-seed drone, a good requirements engineer needs hands-on experience with drones or similar aviation systems — so they can understand each requirement's effect on the drone's overall performance, not just hear the technical detail in isolation.

An important note on communication: a requirements engineer needs to be able to speak both to stakeholders (who usually don't know technical jargon) and to the team's technical experts, each in their own language — and should use layperson terms, not technical ones, whenever talking to stakeholders.

## A few bad traits to avoid

**Expert bias:** many requirements engineers come up through a specific technical field, and unconsciously try to steer the solution toward that field. A good engineer has to set aside their prior expertise and look at the system through the user's/stakeholder's eyes — which is exactly where User Stories and Use Cases (a few lessons ahead) become genuinely useful.

**Impatience:** when waiting on feedback from a slow-to-respond stakeholder, it's tempting to move forward without it. Sometimes that's fine, but if that stakeholder is a key one, moving forward without their input can lead to dramatic, expensive changes later.

**Pride/ego:** a good requirements engineer listens to stakeholders and sees things from their perspective — rather than trying to steer stakeholders toward a solution the engineer already had in mind. Doing this usually produces a suboptimal product.

> Nothing replaces real experience gathering requirements, coordinating across a team, and communicating with people of different backgrounds.
