## Why do we even need requirements?

You might ask: why do we need to elicit, analyze, trace and manage requirements? Why not just hand a developer a bulleted list of features and let them build it?

Short answer: because requirements are fundamentally a **communication tool**. They tell a person or a group what the desired end result of a product or service is. Without a solid set of requirements, a developer can't properly scope the project, and the result is likely to be something half-baked that the user didn't actually want.

## A simple example: building a patio

Say you want to build a patio in your backyard. You're the customer, and you hire a construction contractor. The contractor wants to know:

- How big should the patio be?
- What material should it be built from (brick, stone)?
- Do you want railings or not?

All of these are requirements: **dimensions** is one, **type of stone/brick** is another, **type of railing** is a third.

Requirements aren't always physical or tangible. You might also say the patio needs to be built within 30 days, or that the contractor must pull the proper permit and comply with local building codes, or that any issues must be reported to you. These are requirements too — just non-material ones.

## Requirements are the basis of contracts

More importantly, requirements are the foundation for **contractual agreements** between two parties. In the patio example, the contractor draws up a contract that includes the list of requirements, the estimated time to complete the work, and the agreed payment.

For larger contracts (say, between a government and an aircraft manufacturer), the contract is much bigger and usually split across several separate documents:

| Document | What it describes |
|---|---|
| Performance/functional specification | The end item itself and its characteristics |
| Statement of Work | The work and services to be performed |
| Data requirements list | The documents that must be delivered |
| Terms and conditions | The legal portion of the agreement |

Our legal system (especially Western legal systems) relies on written, formal agreements — meaning that when there's a disagreement, there's a document both parties signed that can be referred back to. If the requirements were written ambiguously or without enough detail, that same document becomes a liability. That's exactly why learning to write *good* requirements matters so much — it's the subject of the next several sections of this course.

## Summary

A well-written, agreed-upon set of requirements is the foundation of the agreement between buyer and seller. The rest of this course is about exactly that: figuring out what your requirements should be, how to write them, and the pitfalls along the way.
